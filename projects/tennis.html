Tennis Shot-Quality Prediction Model

An AI-driven model that scores tennis shot quality on a continuous scale using computer vision and gradient boosting.

Overview

One of the most effective ways for players to improve their tennis performance is by analyzing match footage to identify shots that are poorly executed or fail to achieve the intended impact on the opponent. However, reviewing match footage and documenting each point is extremely time consuming, especially for matches that last hours. When estimating the quality of their shots, players have to manually estimate factors like speed, landing position, and impact on opponents. Having an AI driven model perform this process can efficiently automate this analysis and uncover patterns in a player's game.

This project explores an AI approach to scoring tennis shot quality for individual shots on a continuous scale from zero to ten, utilizing factors such as depth, width, player positions, and speed. By training a highly efficient gradient boosting model (Light GBM) on features extracted from multiple deep learning models, a mean squared error of 0.27, a mean absolute percentage error of 7.31%, and a mean absolute error of 0.44 was achieved.

How It Works

The pipeline extracts numerical data from four primary models working together to analyze broadcast tennis video:

TrackNet CNN Tracks the tennis ball frame-by-frame using a heatmap-based deep learning network, achieving 99.8% precision and 96.6% recall.
Modified TrackNet (Court) Detects 14 key court points and applies a homography matrix to standardize all coordinates onto a 2D reference plane.
Faster R-CNN Detects and tracks both players on the court using bounding boxes, providing precise player position coordinates at every frame.
CatBoost + Binary Moving Average Predicts bounce frames using a pretrained CatBoostRegressor, while a binary moving average model detects hit frames with 90–95% accuracy.

Results

The Light GBM model was trained on 208 labeled shots with an 80-20 train-test split, achieving a mean squared error of 0.27, a mean absolute percentage error of 7.31%, and a mean absolute error of 0.44. The two most important features were ball position X and Y, contributing 22.08% and 22.63% to the model respectively — aligning with the common belief among tennis players that depth and width are the key factors in shot quality.

The model's heatmap output confirmed that shots targeted at wider and deeper regions of the court yield higher scores, while shots aimed toward the center and shallower areas score lower — a pattern consistent with expert tennis knowledge.
